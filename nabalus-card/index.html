There is a plant in the cool woods whose leaves never agree on a single shape. For two hundred years we called it the wrong name. This is for you — and a little about what it taught me.

the round leaf

I

In the beginning your leaf was a whole green coin,
round as a held breath, soft at every edge —
one uncomplicated yes
pressed flat against the light.
You did not yet know
how much of growing is subtraction.

first cuts

II

Then the margins began to think.
Where there had been one smooth shore,
the blade cut inward, took counsel with itself,
and opened the shallow bays of a maple's hand.
This is the first grief no one warns you of:
to become more yourself,
you must give up being simple.

the reaching

III

Deeper now — the lobes divide and reach,
each point a separate weather you came through,
the leaf nearly torn to ribbons by its wanting,
and still: one stem, one root, one rising.
What looked from the outside like coming apart
was only the slow work of coming into.

the true name

IV

They will call you the wrong name for years.
Prenanthes, they said, and meant it kindly —
the way the world names what it half-recognizes.
But you were Nabalus all along, underground:
gall-of-the-earth, white lettuce, rattlesnake-root,
a whole meadow of names for one quiet becoming,
patient while the science caught up to the soul.

the bloom

V

So here is the blessing, late and true:
keep re-cutting your own green outline.
Outgrow the names that no longer fit you
and answer, at last, to the one that does.
Nothing you let go of is lost —
only the round leaf
making room for the many-pointed one.

Happy birthday.

You are the same plant.
You are nothing like before.
Both are how you survived.

— with all my love

A note on the plant

Nabalus is famously heteroblastic: the leaves at a single plant's base, middle, and crown can be so differently shaped that one stem looks like several species. For two centuries these woodland asters were filed under Prenanthes; the North American species have since been moved to Nabalus — a name first proposed back in 1825 and only settled in our own time. The plant never changed. We simply learned to call it by what it always was.
